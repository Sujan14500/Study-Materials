Chapter 1 · Foundations

Learning the rule instead of writing it

Traditional software: you write the rule, the computer applies it. Machine learning: you supply examples of input and output, and the computer works out a rule that fits them. That is the entire difference — and it is only worth it when the rule is too complicated, too fuzzy or too fast-changing to write by hand.

🤔 Should this even be ML?

quiz

The most valuable ML skill is knowing when not to use it. Five real problems — pick before you look.

⚖️
The honest trade. A rule is exact, instant, auditable and free. A model handles complexity no human could enumerate, but it is approximate, needs data, degrades quietly as the world changes, and is hard to explain. Reach for a model when the rule genuinely cannot be written — not because models are more interesting.

Take away

  • Supervised = examples with answers. Unsupervised = structure without answers. Reinforcement = learning from reward.
  • ML pays off where the rule is unwritable, the data already exists, and mistakes are survivable.
  • If a five-line if statement is exactly right, ship the if statement.
Chapter 2 · Foundations

Split it before you touch it

A model's score on data it trained on measures memory, not intelligence. The only honest number comes from data the model has never seen — which is why the split happens first, before scaling, before imputing, before you have even looked at the distributions.

✂️ Train / validation / test

animated

Rough proportions — 60/20/20 is a common default, not a law. With a million rows, 98/1/1 is fine.

🩸
Data leakage is the number one reason a model looks great and then fails. It is information reaching the model that it will not have at prediction time. It never shows up as an error — it shows up as a suspiciously good score. Whenever a result looks too good, look for the leak before you celebrate.

Take away

  • Split first. Everything you fit — scalers, imputers, encoders — learns from the training split only.
  • Touch the test set once. Each extra look converts it into a validation set.
  • Time-ordered data splits on time. Grouped data splits on the group.
  • An unexpectedly great score is a bug report, not a result.
Chapter 3 · Learning

Draw the line, measure the miss

The simplest useful model: y = wx + b. Two numbers. Training means finding the pair that makes the total squared error as small as possible — and there is a closed-form answer, which is exactly why this is the right place to learn what "fitting" means.

📉 Fit it by hand

interactive

24 students. Move the sliders and watch the red residuals — the vertical misses that get squared and averaged into the loss. Get the MSE as low as you can, then press Solve exactly.

mean squared error—

        

          
          
          
        

      

    

  


  

    

      
📐 The loss

      
MSE = (1/n) Σ (yᵢ − (w·xᵢ + b))²

      
Squaring does two jobs: it makes misses in both directions count the same, and it punishes one big miss far more than several small ones. That second property is why least squares is sensitive to outliers — and why MAE (absolute error) is the alternative when your data has a few wild points you cannot remove.

    

    

      
🧮 Why a closed form exists

      
w = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²
b = ȳ − w·x̄

      
The MSE surface for a linear model is a smooth bowl with exactly one bottom, so calculus can solve it directly. Almost no other model is this kind. For everything else — and for linear models with millions of rows — you descend the surface instead, which is the next chapter.

    

  


  

    
Take away

    
    
      
  • A model is parameters plus a loss. Training is search for the parameters that minimise the loss.
    • 
      
  • Residuals are the errors. MSE averages their squares.
    • 
      
  • Linear regression has an exact solution; treat it as your baseline before anything fancier.
    • 
    

  









  

    
Chapter 4 · Learning

    
Walk downhill, carefully

    
Nearly every model you will ever train is fitted by gradient descent: compute which way the loss decreases fastest, take a small step that way, repeat a few thousand times. The only real decision is how big the step is — and getting it wrong is the most common training failure there is.

⚙️ The update rule

      
for each step:
    grad = ∂Loss/∂w          # which way is uphill
    w    = w − lr * grad     # step the other way

      
That is it. The gradient points uphill, so you subtract. lr — the learning rate — scales the step. Everything else in modern optimisation (momentum, Adam, schedules) is refinement on this one line.

    

    

      
📦 Batch, mini-batch, stochastic

      
    
        
  • Batch — use all n rows per step. Smooth, accurate, and impossible above a certain dataset size.
    • 
        
  • Stochastic — one row per step. Very noisy, escapes shallow local minima, rarely used raw.
    • 
        
  • Mini-batch — 32 to 512 rows. The universal default: enough signal to be stable, small enough to fit in memory and use the GPU well.
    • 
      

      
An epoch is one full pass over the data. With 10,000 rows and a batch of 100, one epoch is 100 steps.

    

  


  

    
💥

    
Loss became NaN. Nine times out of ten the learning rate is too high: each step overshoots the minimum by more than it started from, so the error grows geometrically until it overflows. Divide the learning rate by ten and try again. The other one time, you divided by zero or took the log of a negative number.

  


  

    
Take away

    
    
      
  • Gradient descent needs no closed form, so it trains models that have none.
    • 
      
  • Learning rate too low = never finishes. Too high = diverges. Watch the loss curve, not the clock.
    • 
      
  • A healthy loss curve drops steeply, then flattens. Flat from the start means something is wrong.
    • 
    

  









  

    
Chapter 5 · Learning

    
A line that separates, not predicts

    
Regression predicts a number; classification predicts a class. Logistic regression does it by fitting the same linear score, then squashing it through a sigmoid into a probability between 0 and 1. The "decision boundary" is just the place where that probability crosses one half.

🔗 From score to probability

      
z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b        # a linear score
p = 1 / (1 + e^−z)          # sigmoid → 0..1
ŷ = 1 if p ≥ 0.5 else 0     # a threshold

      
The loss is cross-entropy, not MSE: it punishes confident wrong answers enormously and barely notices confident right ones, which is exactly the incentive you want on probabilities.

    

    

      
🚧 When a line is not enough

      
    
        
  • Add features — x², x₁x₂. A line in the new space is a curve in the old one.
    • 
        
  • Kernel SVM — the same trick, done implicitly and efficiently.
    • 
        
  • Trees — carve the space into rectangles instead. No line required.
    • 
        
  • Neural networks — learn the useful transformation rather than guessing it.
    • 
      

      
Try the linear model first. It tells you how much of the problem was easy.

    

  


  

    
Take away

    
    
      
  • Logistic regression is linear regression plus a sigmoid and a different loss.
    • 
      
  • The output is a probability. The 0.5 cut-off is a separate choice — and Chapter 6 is about moving it.
    • 
      
  • Overlapping classes mean some errors are irreducible. Chasing 100% is how you overfit.
    • 
    

  









  

    
Chapter 6 · Judging

    
Accuracy is the worst metric you know

    
A model that predicts "no fraud" for everything is 99.8% accurate on real fraud data and completely worthless. Which metric you choose is a statement about what a mistake costs — and since the two kinds of mistake rarely cost the same, one number is rarely enough.

  • Read the confusion matrix before any summary number. The two error types are different problems.
    • 
      
  • Precision = of what you flagged, how much was right. Recall = of what was real, how much you caught.
    • 
      
  • The threshold is a business decision, not a modelling one. Pick it from the cost of each mistake.

Chapter 7 · Judging

    
The model that memorised the noise

    
Give a model enough flexibility and it will fit your training data perfectly, including the random measurement error in it. That fit is worthless — the noise will be different next time. This is the central tension in all of machine learning, and you can watch it happen in one slider.

🩹 Fixing overfitting

      
    
        
  • More data — the only fix that costs nothing in model quality. Noise averages out; signal does not.
    • 
        
  • Simpler model — fewer parameters, shallower trees, lower degree.
    • 
        
  • Regularisation — L2 shrinks weights toward zero, L1 drives some to exactly zero and does feature selection for free.
    • 
        
  • Early stopping — halt when validation error starts climbing, not when training error stops falling.
    • 
        
  • Fewer features — every extra column is another way to fit noise.
    • 
      

    

    

      
🩹 Fixing underfitting

      
    
        
  • A more flexible model — higher degree, deeper trees, more layers.
    • 
        
  • Better features — usually the biggest win on tabular data. Ratios, interactions, domain knowledge.
    • 
        
  • Less regularisation — you may have clamped it too hard.
    • 
        
  • Train longer — check the loss curve actually flattened before blaming the model.
    • 
      

      
More data does not fix underfitting. If the model cannot represent the shape, another million rows of that shape will not help.

    

  


  

    
📊

    
Diagnose from two numbers. Train error high, test error high → underfitting. Train error low, test error high → overfitting. Both low → you are done. Test error lower than train error → your split is broken, or the test set is unusually easy.

  


  

    
Take away

    
    
      
  • Complexity buys fit on training data and eventually sells generalisation to buy it.
    • 
      
  • Train error alone tells you nothing. The gap is the diagnostic.
    • 
      
  • The best model is the one where held-out error bottoms out, not where training error does.
    • 
    

  









  

    
Chapter 8 · Judging

    
One split is a single sample

    
With 200 rows, a 20% test set is 40 rows — and its score swings by several points depending on which 40 you happened to draw. k-fold rotates the held-out block so every row is tested exactly once, and gives you a mean and a spread instead of one lucky number.

  


  

    
🔁 Five folds
animated

    
Purple is training, green is held out. Watch the held-out block rotate through the dataset.

    

  


  

    
🧭 Which flavour when

    

  


  

    
🔁

    
Cross-validation is not a licence to peek. If you tune hyper-parameters against your CV score and then report that same score, it is optimistic — you selected for it. Nested CV, or a final untouched test set, is what keeps the reported number honest.

  


  

    
Take away

    
    
      
  • k-fold gives a mean and a standard deviation. The spread tells you whether a 1% difference is real.
    • 
      
  • The fold structure must respect the data's structure: stratify on class, group on entity, order on time.
    • 
      
  • Everything you fit goes inside the fold — scalers included, or you have leaked again.
    • 
    

  









  

    
Chapter 9 · Models

    
Twenty questions, chosen greedily

    
A decision tree asks a yes/no question about one feature, splits the data, and repeats. The only clever part is choosing the question — and it does that by brute force: try every threshold, keep the one that separates the classes best. Boosted versions of this are still the best thing on tabular data.

📏 Gini impurity

    
Gini(S) = 1 − Σ pᵢ²

all one class  → 1 − 1² = 0.00   (pure)
50 / 50        → 1 − .5² − .5² = 0.50  (worst)

gain = Gini(parent) − weighted average Gini(children)

    
It is the probability of mislabelling a random sample if you guessed using that group's own class proportions. Zero means the group is pure. Entropy is the near-identical alternative; the choice between them almost never changes the tree.

  


  


  

    
🌲

    
On tabular data, start with gradient boosting. XGBoost, LightGBM or CatBoost on sensible features will beat a neural network on most spreadsheet-shaped problems, train in seconds, and need almost no scaling or encoding work. Deep learning wins on images, audio, text and other unstructured data — which is Deep Learning Fundamentals, next door.

  


  

    
Take away

    
    
      
  • A tree splits greedily on whichever threshold most reduces impurity.
    • 
      
  • One tree is readable and high-variance. Averaging many decorrelated trees fixes the variance.
    • 
      
  • Bagging (forest) reduces variance; boosting reduces bias by correcting previous errors in sequence.
    • 
      
  • Trees need no scaling and handle mixed feature types without ceremony.
    • 
    

  









  

    
Chapter 10 · Models

    
Structure with no answers to check against

    
Unsupervised learning gets inputs and no labels. k-means is the one everybody starts with: pick k centres, assign every point to the nearest one, move each centre to the mean of its points, repeat until nothing moves. Two lines of description, and you can watch every iteration.

🤨 What k-means cannot do

      
    
        
  • Tell you k. Try several, plot inertia, look for the elbow — and then use judgement, because the elbow is often not obvious.
    • 
        
  • Find non-round clusters. It assumes roughly spherical, roughly equal-sized blobs. Use DBSCAN for shapes.
    • 
        
  • Guarantee the best answer. It converges to a local optimum that depends on the starting centres. Run it several times (n_init).
    • 
        
  • Handle unscaled features. It is pure distance — see Chapter 11.
    • 
      

    

    

      
🧪 Judging a clustering

      
There is no accuracy, because there is no ground truth. What you have instead:

      
    
        
  • Inertia — within-cluster spread. Always falls as k rises, so it cannot pick k alone.
    • 
        
  • Silhouette score — how much closer a point is to its own cluster than the next one. Ranges −1 to 1.
    • 
        
  • Does it mean anything? The real test. If the segments do not suggest different actions, the clustering is decoration.
    • 
      

    

  


  

    
Take away

    
    
      
  • Assign, move, repeat. It always converges, and not always to the same place.
    • 
      
  • Choosing k is your job, and the data will not settle it for you.
    • 
      
  • Scale your features first or the widest-ranging column becomes the only one that counts.
    • 
      
  • A clustering is useful when it changes what you do, not when inertia is low.
    • 
    

  









  

    
Chapter 11 · Practice

    
Where the wins actually are

    
On tabular problems, a better feature beats a better algorithm almost every time. This chapter is the unglamorous part of the job that decides whether the project works: getting columns into a form the model can use, and onto a scale where none of them shouts over the others.

  


  

    
⚖️ Standardise it
animated

    
Three features, five customers. Look at the raw bars — income is the only one you can see. Then standardise.

    

    
📐

    

  


  

    
🔧 Turning columns into features

    

  


  

    

      
📊 Standardise or normalise?

      
StandardScaler:  z = (x − mean) / std
   → mean 0, std 1, unbounded
   → the default. Handles outliers better.

MinMaxScaler:    x' = (x − min) / (max − min)
   → squashed into 0..1
   → use when you need a bounded range
   → one outlier crushes everything else

    

    

      
🔒 Do it inside a pipeline

      
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(),
                     LogisticRegression())
pipe.fit(X_train, y_train)     # fits on train only
pipe.score(X_test, y_test)     # transforms test correctly

      
A pipeline makes the leakage bug from Chapter 2 structurally impossible: cross-validation refits the scaler inside every fold automatically. This is the single highest-value habit in the chapter.

    

  


  

    
Take away

    
    
      
  • Distance-based and gradient-based models need scaling. Tree-based models do not care.
    • 
      
  • Fit the scaler on train, transform everything. Put it in a pipeline so you cannot forget.
    • 
      
  • One-hot unordered categories; never label-encode them for a linear model.
    • 
      
  • Ratios, dates broken into components, and a missing-value flag are the cheapest wins available.
    • 
    

  









  

    
Chapter 12 · Practice

    
Nine steps, and the trap in each

    
Real projects fail in predictable places, and almost never at the modelling step. Here is the order that works, with the specific mistake that lies in wait at every stage.

  


  

    
🧭 The loop
animated

    

  


  

    

      
📏 Baselines worth beating

      
regression:      predict the mean
                 predict last week's value
classification:  predict the majority class
                 predict the base rate
time series:     predict yesterday (naive forecast)
recommendation:  most popular item

      
Write the baseline number down before you train anything. It is the only context in which your model's score means anything at all.

    

    

      
🚀 After it ships

      
    
        
  • Log inputs and predictions. Without this you cannot debug or retrain, ever.
    • 
        
  • Watch for drift. Feature distributions move; so does the relationship you fitted.
    • 
        
  • Monitor the outcome, not just the model. The metric that matters is the business one.
    • 
        
  • Plan the retrain. Decide the cadence and trigger before it degrades, not after.
    • 
        
  • Keep the baseline live. If the model ever loses to it, you want to know that day.
    • 
      

    

  


  

    
🎓

    
The whole discipline in one sentence: fit something to data, then work extremely hard to find out whether you fooled yourself. Everything in Chapters 2, 6, 7 and 8 exists for the second half of that sentence.

  


  

    
Take away

    
    
      
  • Frame, split, baseline, simple model, better features — in that order.
    • 
      
  • Tuning is the last 5% of the win and the first 80% of the time spent. Budget accordingly.
    • 
      
  • Every stage has a way to fool yourself. Knowing which one is most of the skill.
    •
