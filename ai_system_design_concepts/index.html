Chapter 3 · Framing it

Every proxy metric is gameable

You cannot train against "user satisfaction", so you pick something measurable that correlates with it. The system then optimises the proxy, including in the ways you did not intend. This is not a modelling problem — it is the central design decision, and the fix is always a guardrail, never a better proxy.

📏 Pick the metric

interactive

Five situations. In each, one option is defensible and the rest are the ones that get chosen in real meetings.

🔗 Offline proxy → online truth

Offline metrics are what you can compute on every commit. Online metrics are what actually happened. The last column is why they disagree.

🎯 A usable metric set

  • One north star. The business outcome. Usually slow to move and hard to attribute — that is fine, it is the thing you actually want.
  • One or two proxies. Fast-moving, trainable, and known to be gameable. This is what the model optimises.
  • Three to five guardrails. Latency, error rate, complaint rate, revenue, coverage. Metrics you refuse to regress even for a north-star win.
  • A segment breakdown. Aggregate wins routinely hide a destroyed minority segment — new users, the query tail, one market.

⚠️ Goodhart, concretely

  • Optimise CTR → you get clickbait. The model found the cheapest way to earn a click.
  • Optimise watch time → you get autoplay and long videos, whether or not anyone wanted them.
  • Optimise resolution rate → the assistant stops escalating and people suffer politely.
  • Optimise "helpful" ratings → you get flattery, because agreeable answers rate well.
  • Optimise recall on fraud → you block half your good customers to catch one more fraudster.
🛡️
The pattern is always the same, and so is the fix: pair every proxy with the guardrail that catches its specific abuse. Never expect a better proxy to save you.
Chapter 4 · Data

Your labels are someone's behaviour, not the truth

A click is not "this was relevant" — it is "this was first, and the thumbnail was interesting". Every label source trades volume against bias against cost, and choosing one is choosing which systematic error your model will learn.

🏷️ Five sources, three trade-offs

🪤 Five traps in label data

🎲
The cheapest thing you can do for label quality is randomise a tiny slice of traffic — 1% shown in random order — and log it. That slice is unbiased by your current model, which makes it the only data you can trust for measuring position bias, evaluating a new ranker offline, and answering "would users have liked the thing we never showed them". It costs a fraction of a percent of engagement and it is worth more than any modelling change.
Chapter 5 · Data

The two bugs that make a great model useless

Leakage makes your offline metric a lie. Training/serving skew makes your production metric a disappointment. Both are silent, both are common, and both are found by asking one question of every feature: when does this value actually become known?

🔍 Judge the feature

interactive

Eight features from a real-ish churn and ranking model. Safe, skewed, or leaking? Two of them are genuinely nasty.

⏱️ Point-in-time correctness

The single most important idea in feature engineering, and the one most often got wrong by a plain SQL join:

-- ✗ leaks the future: today's value on a 2024 row
SELECT e.*, u.total_spend
FROM   events e JOIN users u USING (user_id);

-- ✓ as-of join: the value as it was at event time
SELECT e.*, f.total_spend
FROM   events e
ASOF JOIN user_features f
  ON  f.user_id = e.user_id
  AND f.valid_at <= e.event_time;

If your feature store does not do this for you, you are doing it by hand on every training run — and you will forget once.

🛠️ Five fixes

📝
The one habit that removes this whole chapter's problems: log the exact feature vector you served with every prediction, and train on those logs. Skew becomes impossible by construction, because training data is by definition what serving produced. It costs you storage and it saves you an incident per quarter.
Chapter 6 · Modelling

Climb only as far as the metric justifies

Five rungs, each roughly an order of magnitude more expensive to serve than the last. The right rung is not the highest one you can build — it is the lowest one that clears the bar. Most teams overshoot by two, and pay for it every day in serving cost.

📌 Five rules

🔄 The LLM inverts the economics

Classic ML is expensive to build and cheap to run. LLMs are the reverse, and that changes where each one wins:

  • High volume, narrow task → classic ML. 40,000 QPS of ticket classification through an LLM is an absurd bill for a task logistic regression handles.
  • Low volume, high variety → LLM. Fifty different one-off extraction tasks are fifty models you will never build, and one prompt each.
  • Prototype up, serve down. Use an LLM to prove the task is solvable and to generate labels; distil into a small model for serving. This is the highest-leverage pattern on the ladder.
  • No labels at all → LLM, always. It is the only rung that works from zero, which is often the whole argument.
Chapter 7 · Serving

You cannot score a million items. Nobody can.

So every large ranking system has the same shape: a cheap stage that cuts millions to hundreds optimising only for recall, a real model that ranks those, and an expensive model over the final few dozen. Roughly 100× fewer items and 100× more compute per item at each step.

📌 Five things about funnels

🧊 What gets precomputed

The request path should compute as little as it possibly can. Everything else moves offline:

  • Item embeddings — nightly batch. Items change far more slowly than requests arrive.
  • The ANN index — rebuilt on a schedule, with incremental upserts for fresh items.
  • User embeddings — batch for the long tail, recomputed online only for users with fresh activity.
  • Whole ranked feeds — for inactive users, computed offline and served from a cache. They are most of your users and almost none of your traffic.
  • What cannot be precomputed: anything depending on the current request — session context, the query itself, real-time inventory.
Chapter 8 · Serving

Spend the budget like money

You have an SLO. Every component takes a slice, and the slices must add up to less than it — at p99, not p50. Design the budget before you design the system and the architecture largely writes itself, because most options are already ruled out.

📌 Five things about tails

✂️
When you are over budget, in order of what to try: move work offline (precompute), cut the work per request (smaller candidate set, smaller model), parallelise what is independent, cache aggressively, and only then optimise code. The first two usually win by an order of magnitude; the last one wins by 20% after a fortnight.
Chapter 9 · Serving

Arithmetic you should be able to do in your head

Users × requests ÷ seconds × peak factor = QPS. Concurrency ÷ latency = QPS per replica. Those two lines answer most capacity questions in a design conversation, and getting them wrong by 10× is the difference between a service and an outage.

📌 Five things about scaling

🔪 When one box is not enough

  • Replicate stateless things. Model servers and API nodes scale horizontally for free. Do this first, always.
  • Shard stateful things. A 200M-document index does not fit on one machine — shard by document, scatter the query, gather the results.
  • Scatter-gather has a tail cost. Querying 20 shards means waiting for the slowest of 20. Hedge, or accept partial results after a deadline.
  • Replicate each shard too. Sharding is for capacity; replication is for availability. You need both, and the multiplication is your real machine count.
  • Route by tenant when you can. It bounds the blast radius of one noisy customer, and it makes per-tenant limits enforceable.

🧊 The caching hierarchy

  • Result cache — the whole response, keyed by a normalised request. Highest value, hardest to invalidate.
  • Feature cache — user and item features. Bounded staleness is nearly always acceptable and nobody notices.
  • Embedding cache — text → vector. Deterministic and immutable, so it never needs invalidating. Free money.
  • Model output cache — for LLMs especially, where identical prompts are common and each miss is expensive.
  • Negative caching — remember "no results" too, or your most expensive queries are the ones that find nothing.

The system that poisons its own training data

A recommender only sees feedback for what it showed. Items it never shows get no clicks, look bad, and get shown even less. Nothing alerts, no metric goes red — the catalogue just quietly collapses onto whatever was popular the week you launched.

Chapter 12 · LLM systems

Retrieval quality is the ceiling

If the right chunk never reaches the prompt, no model and no prompt wording produces the right answer. Most "the model hallucinated" incidents are retrieval failures wearing a generation costume — and they are cheaper to fix once you know that.

🎛️ RAG knobs, and what they cost

interactive

📌 Six things about RAG in production

🏗️ The indexing pipeline

  • Ingest → parse → chunk → embed → upsert. Every stage needs to be re-runnable for one document, not just for the whole corpus.
  • Content hash per chunk. Re-embed only what changed. Full rebuilds get expensive fast at 200k documents.
  • Tombstone deletes. A retracted document that stays in the index is the worst kind of bug — confidently wrong, correctly cited.
  • Version the embedding model. Changing it invalidates the whole index. Plan a dual-write and a backfill, not a swap.
  • Keep metadata on every chunk. Source, tenant, product, date. It powers filters, citations and "why did we answer that".

🚨 Failure modes specific to RAG

  • Retrieved-but-ignored. The right chunk was in the prompt and the model answered from priors anyway. Check groundedness, not just recall.
  • Stale index. Correct answer, out of date. Alert on index lag, not just on errors.
  • Tenant bleed. A missing filter returns another customer's document. Enforce the filter server-side; never trust the caller to pass it.
  • Injection via documents. Anything a user can edit and the system later retrieves is an instruction channel. Treat retrieved text as data.
  • Silent truncation. The context exceeded the window and the retrieved chunks were the part that got dropped. Log the rendered prompt length.
Chapter 13 · LLM systems

Nobody searches all ten million vectors

The last chapter assumed the right chunk comes back. This is the machinery that makes that affordable. Every vector index is the same idea in different clothes: group the vectors up front so a query can ignore almost all of them. Group them with k-means and you have IVF; wire them into a graph and you have HNSW. The speed and the wrongness come from the same decision — the vectors you deliberately refuse to look at.

🕸️ HNSW: coarse hops first

animated

The other way to look at less. Instead of cells, a layered graph: the top layer has a few nodes with very long links, and each layer below has more nodes and shorter ones. The search hops greedily toward the query on each layer, then drops a level and continues from where it stopped.

📌 Six things about vector indexes in production

🧭
Pick the index with a multiplication, not a benchmark. bytes-per-vector × vectors × replicas tells you whether you are choosing between HNSW and IVF-Flat at all, or whether compression was decided for you before the conversation started. Then, and only then, tune recall — with nprobe if you built IVF, with efSearch if you built HNSW, and always against exact search over a golden set, because "the results got worse" is not a number anyone can act on.
Chapter 14 · LLM systems

Where the money and the milliseconds go

LLM systems have an unusual cost curve: per-request, variable, and dominated by tokens you often did not need to send. The three levers that matter are routing traffic to the cheapest model that can handle it, caching aggressively, and keeping the prompt small.

🧠 The KV cache ceiling

interactive

Self-hosting sizing question everyone gets wrong the first time: how many users can one GPU serve at once? People reach for the weights. The weights are a fixed cost you pay once. What scales with every concurrent request — and what actually runs you out of memory — is the KV cache: the attention keys and values for every token in flight. ⏱️ Where a request's time actually goes

interactive

A request is two jobs with different bottlenecks. Prefill pushes the whole prompt through in one parallel pass. Decode emits one token per forward pass and cannot be parallelised. Each lever below attacks exactly one of them — click to see which number moves, and which stubbornly does not.

🚫 What each lever does not do

The expensive mistake is reaching for the wrong one of these, then spending a sprint being surprised that nothing moved.

🔧 Eight levers

💸 Where the tokens actually go

  • Retrieved context is usually the largest line in a RAG bill, and k is the knob nobody tunes.
  • Conversation history grows every turn. Turn 20 resends turns 1–19 unless you trim or summarise.
  • The system prompt is small but paid on every single request. Cache the prefix.
  • Output tokens cost several times input. "Be concise" is a genuine cost optimisation, not just a style note.
  • Retries and agent loops multiply everything above. Cap them in code.

📊 What to put on the dashboard

  • Cost per request, p50 and p95. The mean hides the runaway loop that spends your month in a night.
  • Tokens in / out per request, broken down by prompt section. This is where you find the 4k-token system prompt nobody meant to ship.
  • Cache hit rate per cache layer. A hit rate that drops is usually a prefix that stopped being stable.
  • Time to first token, not just total latency. It is what users actually perceive.
  • Escalation and retry rate. Both are quality signals disguised as cost signals.
  • Spend per tenant, with caps enforced in code well before the invoice arrives.

📌 Five operating rules

🪤
Redis is a copy, not a source of truth. It is one process holding your data in memory, and the honest design assumption is that the whole thing can disappear between two requests. Every use above survives that: a cold cache is slow, not wrong; a lost leaderboard rebuilds from orders; a lost lock is a lease that expired; a lost geo index refills on the next round of pings. The moment something in Redis cannot be recomputed from the database, you have quietly promoted a cache to a database — and it is a database with no schema, no transactions worth the name, and an eviction policy that deletes your data on purpose.
